What does the Mann–Whitney effect mean?

The illustration reports P(B > A) + ½P(B = A): the chance that a randomly selected B respondent rates higher than an independently selected A respondent, with ties counting half. A value of 50% means no directional rank advantage. This is not a 50% T2B score, a paired improvement probability, or a mean difference. Different distributions can share 50%; the planner will not produce a directional sample size in that case.

BEHIND THE NUMBER

A sample size is a set of choices.

Precision or power?

Estimation asks how closely you can measure a value. Comparisons ask whether you can detect a meaningful difference. Power belongs to the second question.

Who counts as one person?

Independent comparisons need separate groups. Paired scale comparisons use the same people twice and require the SD of their differences. Several items from one person are not independent participants.

Plan for your audience.

These calculations assume independent participants from the population of interest. Larger samples do not fix recruitment bias. Plan separately for segments you need to report.

Formulas, sources & scope

This prototype uses the research scenarios in Lewis & Sauro (2016), Excel and R Companion to Quantifying the User Experience, chapters 6–7. It is an independent implementation; normal approximations can differ from the book’s t-based, adjusted-Wald, and N−1 methods.

  • Discovery: n = ceiling[log(1 − target) / log(1 − occurrence)].
  • Rate precision: unrounded n₀ = z²p(1 − p) / margin². Optional finite population correction: n = ceiling[N n₀ / (N − 1 + n₀)], assuming simple random sampling without replacement from N eligible people. This applies only to rate estimation in the sample-size tab; other tools do not inherit the correction.
  • Mean precision: n = ceiling[(z × SD / margin)²].
  • Independent means: n per group = ceiling[2(z + z_power)² × SD² / difference²].
  • Paired means: n = ceiling[(z + z_power)² × SD_difference² / difference²].
  • Independent rates: n per group = ceiling[(z√(2p̄(1−p̄)) + z_power√(p₁(1−p₁)+p₂(1−p₂)))² / (p₂−p₁)²].
  • Fixed benchmark proportion: n = ceiling[(z√(p₀(1−p₀)) + z_power√(p₁(1−p₁)))² / (p₁−p₀)²]. For a two-sided test this is a dominant-tail approximation; the opposite rejection tail is omitted.
  • Ordinal Mann–Whitney (equal allocation): N = (z + z_power)²[1 − Σ((pᵢ+qᵢ)/2)³] / [3(θ−0.5)²], where θ = P(B > A) + ½P(B = A). Each group is rounded up from N/2. This is the tie-adjusted asymptotic approximation in Zhao et al. (2008), Equation 12, not an exact power calculation.
  • Fixed benchmark mean: n = ceiling[((z + z_power) × SD / |expected − benchmark|)²].
  • Estimation uses two-sided intervals. Tests offer one or two sides; “good enough” uses a one-sided above-target test. z = Φ⁻¹(1 − α_effective / tails).
  • Sample-size calculations use the per-test α you select, with no automatic family adjustment. The separate multiple-comparisons guide explains analysis choices; it does not power corrected tests.
  • Independent groups have equal allocation. Recruitment inflation is rounded separately within each group. Family corrections are not applied to these recruitment targets.

The ordinal mode uses its own tie-adjusted rank approximation. Mean and proportion modes use normal approximations, which may underestimate sample sizes for small studies; mean formulas assume approximately normal means and use a planning SD. Skewed task times need a justified transformation and precision defined on that scale. This version does not calculate paired binary outcomes, clustered designs, non-inferiority / equivalence, ordinal regression, paired rank tests, or joint power. Benchmark calculations treat the reference as fixed. Family-wise error control concerns false positives; 80% per-effect power is not 80% power to detect every effect. For multiple outcomes collected from the same people, calculate each and generally use the largest sample; segment-level conclusions may require that sample in each segment.

Background: NIST: selecting sample sizes and NIST: confidence limits for the mean. For a final small-sample plan, use an appropriate exact or t-based procedure.

SEPARATE ANALYSIS GUIDE

Doing several comparisons?

The sample-size calculator above uses unadjusted, per-test error rates. This guide helps you choose an analysis strategy. Its settings do not change the sample-size result; corrections can increase the sample needed for the same power.

What counts as a family?

A family is a group of tests for which you want to limit the chance of one or more false-positive claims together. It is defined by the research question and how you will use the results, rather than automatically by the whole dataset, the survey, or whether tests are correlated. Researchers can reasonably choose different families for different inferential goals; state and justify your choice before examining results.

One decision, several ways to win

You test whether a redesign improves ease, trust, and usefulness, then call it successful if any one improves. Those tests naturally belong in a shared family, even though the constructs differ.

Related comparisons

You compare four concepts on the same usefulness item to identify which differ. All six pairwise contrasts are a sensible family. A single pre-specified A-versus-B contrast is a different plan.

Distinct hypotheses

A checkout hypothesis and an unrelated account-recovery hypothesis may justify separate families in the same dataset. Similar tests are a useful clue, but the claims and decision rules matter more than the file they share.

Do not split a family after seeing the p-values to preserve significance. A priori comparisons are specified in advance; several can still belong to one family. A rule requiring every co-primary outcome to pass has different error-control implications from declaring success when any passes.

Which approach fits the claim?

SituationApproach to consider
One pre-specified primary testIts own per-test α; distinguish secondary and exploratory analyses.
A defined family of confirmatory claimsHolm controls family-wise error under arbitrary dependence, given valid p-values, and is no less powerful than Bonferroni. Bonferroni is simpler and also supports simultaneous intervals.
Every pair of group meansTukey / Tukey–Kramer when its model assumptions apply; for repeated measures use a model and contrasts that account for pairing.

Illustrate thresholds for a family you define

Holm: order p-values smallest to largest, compare sequentially to α/(m−i+1), and stop rejecting at the first failure. Bonferroni: compare each p-value to α/m. Neither procedure requires independent tests. This guide does not analyze p-values or calculate corrected power.

Sources: NIST: multiple-comparison methods; NIST: Bonferroni; R statistical documentation: Holm and FDR adjustments.

What is a difference worth detecting?

It is the smallest population difference that would matter to your research or product decision. For example, a change from 3.5 to 3.8 is 0.3 points on a 1–5 scale. Whether that is useful depends on the construct, consequences, cost of change, and relevant evidence; 0.3 is only an illustration.

Choose this value before data collection. The calculator finds a sample with the selected power to detect an effect of that size against zero. It does not guarantee significance, and a significant result alone does not establish practical importance or prove that the effect exceeds your meaningful threshold. Interpret the estimated difference and its confidence interval against the decision-relevant value.

Can I assume SD = 1.1 on a five-point scale?

It is a plausible starting assumption for an individual 1–5 item, not a ceiling. MeasuringU found an average SD of about 1.01 across five-point items in its dataset. A population split equally between 1 and 5 has SD 2. Use pilot or comparable historical data when possible, and check how a larger SD changes recruitment. The SD of a multi-item composite depends on its scoring and correlations; paired comparisons require the SD of within-person differences.

Source: MeasuringU: variability of UX rating scales.
